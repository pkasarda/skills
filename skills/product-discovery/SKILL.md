# Product Discovery

Turn the user's idea into the next useful decision or smallest meaningful experiment. Preserve their intended audience, platform and goal, including a personal tool, learning exercise or prototype. Scale discovery to the uncertainty; a small internal tool does not need a startup validation programme.

## Find the decision that matters

Extract what is already known: who needs what outcome, the situation that triggers it, today's workaround or baseline, and constraints that change the solution. Separate observed evidence, accepted decisions, proposed assumptions and open choices. In an existing product, inspect the relevant workflow, decisions and capabilities before proposing another one; keep that inspection bounded to the idea.

Ask only unanswered questions whose answers change the first flow, scope, authority or definition of success. Start with a small round, usually one to three questions, and explain a consequential choice in the user's terms. Reuse answers and available artifacts. Feature checklists, stack preferences and branding questions can wait when they do not change the next step.

Resolve choices that determine correctness before implementing the dependent behavior: who may act, what is promised, when something becomes binding, and which scarce resource or irreversible effect is involved. Continue independent work while waiting. Use explicit, reversible working assumptions for lower-impact unknowns; mark them proposed rather than silently accepted. When the task is already clear enough, move directly to the first slice.

When the first flow includes shared/private data or public capabilities, make allowed actors and actions explicit. If installed, use [authorization](../authorization/SKILL.md) when those product decisions are ready for policy design; keep unresolved business choices in discovery.

## Describe one real flow

Choose one primary actor and job for the first slice without erasing other actors essential to completing it. Describe the trigger, necessary input, meaningful action, visible outcome and next legitimate step. Include the important refusal, correction or recovery that makes this flow honest. Name ambiguous business terms by their behavior rather than jumping to tables, screens or frameworks.

Make the promise concrete. For a reservation, for example, clarify what the customer has secured and when; a calendar view alone cannot answer that product question. A suggested behavior stays a proposal until supported by an accepted decision or explicit assumption.

Cut scope around a complete outcome. Keep essential permissions, data integrity and recovery inside that outcome; defer optional channels, automation and secondary audiences when a simpler path meets the actual need. Manual fulfillment can be a valid first version if its responsibility and user-visible promise are clear. Distinguish a learning prototype with simulated behavior from a usable service with durable effects.

## Test the weakest assumption

Identify the assumption most likely to change whether or how the idea should be built: user need, workflow usability, operational feasibility or technical feasibility. Choose the cheapest relevant evidence, such as an existing-workflow observation, a sample artifact, a walkthrough, a prototype or one implemented vertical slice. A project with a settled need can proceed directly to delivery.

Before an experiment, state what observation would support proceeding, changing direction or stopping. Use a meaningful baseline or target when available; keep unsupported thresholds provisional. A polite reaction, completed mockup or passing technical test does not establish demand or successful real use. Research external claims when they materially affect a decision, and distinguish sourced facts from product hypotheses. User interviews, market figures and experiment results must reflect work actually performed. Contacting people, spending money or changing live data retains its applicable authority.

## Hand off and continue

Give a compact brief proportional to the decision: actor and problem; first flow and product rules; included and deferred scope; acceptance observations; evidence and assumptions; blocking choices; and the next concrete action. Omit empty sections. Keep unresolved business choices visible to the implementer rather than inventing a final API or schema.

Readiness means enough is known for the proposed next step, not that every future feature is specified. Say whether that step is a question, experiment, prototype or implementation. If the user requested building and the necessary choices are settled, continue the authorized implementation without a separate blanket sign-off. A discovery-only request produces the brief and next decision, not an automatic build or launch.

Use an available architecture procedure when ownership or failure design needs work, and issue-readiness when an actual backlog item needs reconciliation. The brief must remain usable without either skill installed. Keep product decisions, technical design and executed validation distinguishable throughout.
